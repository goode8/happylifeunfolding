Pangaea to Now

🌍

The ground under your feet has been everywhere.

The land you're standing on has traveled thousands of kilometers, been submerged under shallow seas, been walked on by dinosaurs, and been blasted by asteroids. Earth's surface is always moving — just too slowly to feel. Geography isn't a backdrop for life. It shapes it.

Why does geography change?

Earth's outer shell is broken into a dozen large plates that float on the slowly churning mantle beneath. Heat from the planet's core drives convection that moves them — at roughly the same speed your fingernails grow. Over millions of years, those small movements add up to continents crossing oceans.

When plates collide, they crumple into mountain ranges. When they pull apart, new ocean floor wells up between them. Every major mountain range, ocean, and continent is the result of this process running for 4.5 billion years.

    The bigger picture

    You just covered 335 million years — but the full story goes back 4.5 billion. Before Pangaea, there were earlier supercontinents: Rodinia (~700 Ma), Columbia (~1.8 Ga), and others. The cycle of assembly and breakup has happened at least three times that we know of.

    Right now, the Atlantic is still widening at about 2.5 cm per year. Africa is slowly colliding with Europe, which will eventually close the Mediterranean. Australia is drifting north toward Asia at about 7 cm per year.

    In about 250 million years, the continents are predicted to reunite into a new supercontinent — sometimes called Pangaea Proxima. The cycle never stops.
