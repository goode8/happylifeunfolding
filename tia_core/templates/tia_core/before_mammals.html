Before Mammals

🦴

Before the first dinosaur existed,
one lineage was already changing.

About 300 million years ago, a group called synapsids diverged from other vertebrates. Over the next 90 million years, inherited variations that happened to aid survival kept accumulating — different tooth shapes, signs of fur, changes to the jaw and ear. Those changes eventually produced every mammal alive today, including you.

Are these our ancestors?

Most are not direct ancestors — they're relatives. The synapsid family had many branches, and most died out. Five of the six animals here belong to branches that went extinct. Morganucodon is close to the lineage that survived to become modern mammals.

All of their fossils are valuable because they preserve the same kinds of changes that were spreading across many synapsid lineages at the time — shifts in tooth structure, evidence of fur, changes to the jaw and skull. Each one spread because, over many generations, it happened to improve survival or reproduction.

These six animals are snapshots of that process at different moments in time.

The bigger picture

You just met six animals. But the synapsid family has thousands more — scientists have identified over a thousand different species so far, with more being discovered as fossil research continues.

Lystrosaurus belonged to a group called dicynodonts — that group alone had over 200 species. Inostrancevia's group, the gorgonopsians, had nearly 80. For tens of millions of years, before a single dinosaur existed, synapsids were the most common large animals on land.

And here's the thing: the family never died out. Every mammal alive today is a synapsid. That includes dogs, whales, bats — and you.
